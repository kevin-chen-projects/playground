The recipe book for every living thing.

From the moss on a tree to the yeast in your bread to you — all of it is written in the same 4-letter alphabet.

How does DNA get out of the nucleus?

DNA is precious. The cell never lets the original leave the nucleus. But proteins get built outside, where the ribosomes are.

So the cell makes a working copy. A short, single-stranded version of just the gene it needs. That copy is called mRNA, and the process is transcription.

Module 3 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

📚

🤖
The copy machine

A protein called RNA polymerase. Reads DNA, builds RNA.

📋
The copy (mRNA)

Single strand. Just one gene. Disposable.

🚪
The exit (nuclear pore)

mRNA slips out through tiny doors in the nuclear membrane.

Scene 3 of 6 · Notice: U replaces T

DNA vs. RNA — what's different?

Both store info. Both are made of base letters. But they have different jobs and a few important differences:

🧬 DNA

  • Two strands, twisted (the famous helix)
  • Letters: A, T, G, C
  • Long-term storage — the master file
  • Stays in the nucleus
  • Lasts your whole lifetime

📝 RNA (specifically, mRNA)

  • Single strand, no twist
  • Letters: A, U, G, C (U replaces T)
  • Disposable — a working copy
  • Travels to the ribosomes
  • Lasts minutes to hours, then recycled

Now we actually build the protein.

You have an mRNA strand sitting next to a ribosome outside the nucleus. The ribosome doesn't read English. It reads codons: 3-letter chunks of mRNA.

Each codon picks one amino acid. The amino acids snap together into a chain. The chain folds up — and that's a working protein.

Why is a brain cell nothing like a skin cell?

Every cell in your body — your eye cells, your gut cells, your neurons, your bone cells — has the exact same DNA. The same complete set of recipes.

But they look completely different and do completely different jobs. The trick: each cell only turns on the recipes it actually needs. Today we look at the on/off switches.

💪

Turns on stretchy-fiber genes (actin/myosin) and lots of mitochondria.

🛡️
Skin cell

Turns on keratin (a tough protein) and genes for fast cell division.

🍷

Turns on toxin-breakdown genes and sugar-storage genes.

Scene 3 of 6 · How are switches flipped?

What flips the switches?

Cells have a few clever ways to turn genes on and off. The most important one: transcription factors — proteins whose whole job is to slide along the DNA and decide which genes get copied next.

🔑
Transcription factors

Proteins that bind near a gene and either invite or block RNA polymerase.

🚦
Promoter region

A short stretch of DNA right before each gene — where regulators bind.

💊

🌅 Your DNA isn't your destiny.

Coming up next: Mutations & Evolution

Now that you know the system, let's see what happens when it glitches — and how those glitches, over millions of years, drive the entire history of life on Earth.

Module 5 complete · One more in the foundations arc
~70
brand-new mutations the average newborn carries — that neither parent had.

Tiny typos. Long-term consequences.

Every time a cell copies its DNA, it makes a few mistakes. Most of those typos do absolutely nothing — the cell rolls on like it never happened. Sometimes a typo changes one ingredient in one recipe. Once in a while, it stops a recipe in the middle.

Stack those rare changes up across millions of years and billions of organisms, and you get the entire diversity of life on Earth — from algae to elephants to you.

Module 6 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

Three flavors of typo.

When one DNA letter gets swapped for another, it lands in a codon — a 3-letter "word" the ribosome reads. Depending on which word the typo creates, the consequences range from nothing to everything.

🟢
Silent

The codon changed, but the new codon codes for the same amino acid. Protein unchanged. The genetic code's "redundancy" is doing you a favor.

🟡
Missense

The new codon codes for a different amino acid. The protein gets one wrong ingredient. Sometimes harmless, sometimes catastrophic.

🔴
Nonsense

The new codon is a stop signal. The ribosome quits early. The protein is short and almost always broken.

⏳
+ Time + Selection

Tiny advantages from rare beneficial mutations spread. Over millions of years, that's evolution. No designer needed.

Scene 3 of 6 · One famous example

One letter, two stories.

In the gene for hemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in your red blood cells), there's a codon that reads GAG — Glutamic acid. Sometimes a copy error changes that middle letter to a U: GUG — Valine. One letter. One amino acid. Huge consequences.

Two copies of the mutation

Sickle cell disease. Under stress, hemoglobin clumps and bends red blood cells into rigid crescents that get stuck in tiny vessels. Painful, sometimes fatal.

One copy of the mutation

Mostly fine — and partially resistant to malaria. The malaria parasite has a much harder time inside the sickle-shaped cells.

Zero copies

Normal hemoglobin, normal red cells. Fully susceptible to malaria — which is why, in places where malaria is common, carriers actually live longer on average.

🎯 Mutations aren't bad → good. They're traded off.

The sickle-cell mutation looks "bad" in places without malaria, and "good" in places with it. Selection doesn't pick perfect — it picks whatever survives long enough to reproduce. Over millions of generations, that's all evolution is.

Coming up next: Cancer & Genetic Disease

Most mutations are harmless. Sometimes, though, the wrong mutations stack up in the wrong cells, in the wrong order — the cell forgets to follow the rules and just keeps growing. We call that cancer. (Coming soon!)

Module 6 complete · 5 of 11 unlocked, 6 to go
1 in 3
people will get cancer in their lifetime. Where does it come from?

Cancer is what happens when the recipes get scrambled.

You already know cells follow DNA recipes. Cancer is what happens when key recipes get damaged — specifically, the recipes that tell a cell when to stop growing and when to die.

It isn't a single disease. It's hundreds of different diseases that all share one pattern: a cell forgets the rules and starts copying itself when it shouldn't.

Module 7 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

Cancer cells break four rules.

Healthy cells are obedient. They grow when needed, stop when crowded, and quietly self-destruct when damaged. Cancer cells defy all four:

📈
Grow without permission

Normal cells wait for "go" signals from neighbors. Cancer cells produce their own.

🚫
Ignore "stop" signals

Normal cells stop dividing when crowded. Cancer cells keep going.

⏳
Refuse to die

Damaged cells normally self-destruct (apoptosis). Cancer cells disable this.

🩸
Build their own blood supply

Growing tumors trick the body into sprouting new blood vessels to feed them.

Scene 3 of 6 · Which genes break?

Two kinds of broken genes.

Cancer-causing mutations hit two categories of genes: the gas pedals and the brakes of cell division. Each behaves differently when broken.

🏎️
Oncogenes (gas pedals)

Normally tell the cell "grow now". Cancer happens when they get stuck ON. One broken copy is enough — like a stuck accelerator. Examples: RAS, MYC, HER2.

🛑
Tumor suppressors (brakes)

Normally tell the cell "stop" or "die if damaged". Cancer happens when they both copies break. Two-hit rule — like losing brakes AND emergency brake. Examples: p53, BRCA1/2, RB.

💡 Why p53 is called "the guardian of the genome".

p53 detects DNA damage. If damage is fixable, it pauses the cell to fix. If not, it orders apoptosis (self-destruction). Roughly half of all human cancers have a broken p53. When the guardian sleeps, damage accumulates.

Cancer in one sentence.

Cancer is what happens when a cell accumulates enough mutations in the right genes (oncogenes ON + tumor suppressors OFF) that it forgets the body's growth rules and starts copying itself unchecked.

Coming up next: Viruses

Cancer is your own cells betraying you. Viruses are something else entirely — outsiders that hijack your cells to make copies of themselves. Let's meet them.

Module 7 complete · Disease arc continues
10³¹
viruses on Earth — more than every star in the universe.

Viruses are biology's freeloaders.

A virus isn't really alive. It has DNA or RNA wrapped in a protein shell, and that's it — no cells, no metabolism, no way to reproduce on its own.

But give it a living cell to hijack, and it becomes a copy machine. It turns the cell's machinery against itself, makes hundreds of new virus particles, and bursts out to find more cells.

Module 8 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

The basic anatomy of a virus.

Strip a virus down to its essentials. There are only a few parts — and not every virus has all of them.

🧬
Genetic payload

DNA or RNA — usually just a few thousand to a few hundred thousand bases. Tiny.

📦
Capsid (protein shell)

A geometric protein cage that protects the genome until it finds a host.

🛢️
Envelope (sometimes)

A fatty outer layer stolen from a previous host cell. Influenza and HIV have one; many others don't.

🔑
Surface keys

Spike proteins that fit specific lock receptors on host cells — what determines which cells the virus can infect.

Scene 3 of 6 · How viruses vary

Not all viruses are alike.

The category mostly comes down to what's inside the capsid and how that genetic payload gets copied. These three are the main families for human disease.

📘
DNA viruses

Carry DNA. Use the host's normal copy machinery. Slow to mutate (good copying). Examples: herpes, chickenpox, HPV, smallpox.

📕
RNA viruses

Carry RNA. Use a sloppy enzyme (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) that copies fast but makes errors. Fast mutators. Examples: flu, cold, measles, COVID, hepatitis A/C, polio.

📒
Retroviruses

Carry RNA but reverse-transcribe it into DNA, then insert into your genome permanently. Examples: HIV. About 8% of human DNA came from ancient retroviruses long ago.

🌊 Why flu changes every year.

Flu is an RNA virus. Each replication round, the sloppy enzyme makes a few errors. Most are deleterious — but rare ones change the surface keys enough that last year's antibodies don't recognize this year's virus. That's why you need a new flu shot annually.

Coming up next: The Immune System

Viruses are constantly trying to infect you. So why aren't you sick all the time? Because your body has a small army of cells whose whole job is to find and destroy them. Let's meet that army.

Module 8 complete
10¹¹
lymphocytes patrolling your body right now — 100 billion soldiers.

Your body has a built-in security force.

Every second, you breathe in thousands of microbes. Some could kill you. But you don't notice — because hidden inside you, an enormous distributed army is doing nothing but identifying threats.

This is the immune system. It has two layers that work together: a fast generalist (innate) and a slow specialist (adaptive).

Module 9 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

Two layers of defense.

Most invaders never get past the first layer. The second layer only shows up if the first one can't finish the job — but when it does, it brings precision weapons.

⚡
Innate (fast generalist)

Born with it. Responds to anything "not me" in minutes. Skin, mucus, fever, macrophages. No memory.

🎯
Adaptive (slow specialist)

Takes 7–14 days to ramp up. Makes custom weapons. Has memory. This is what vaccines target.

🤝
They cooperate

Innate cells show samples of invaders to adaptive cells, which design precision weapons.

🧠
Memory is the magic

After the first fight, memory cells stay for years. Next time: hours, not days.

Scene 3 of 6 · The precision weapons

The two stars of adaptive immunity.

B-cells and T-cells are the snipers of the immune system. Both learn to recognize one specific invader and ignore everything else.

📤
B-cells → antibodies

When a B-cell finds its target, it pumps out antibodies: Y-shaped proteins that latch onto invaders and mark them for destruction. Best against extracellular threats (bacteria, virus particles in blood).

🗡️
T-cells → kill infected cells

Helper T-cells coordinate the response. Killer T-cells destroy your own cells that have been infected with a virus. Best against intracellular threats and cancer.

📚 How does the body have a cell that recognizes any invader?

The trick is genetic recombination. Each developing B-cell and T-cell randomly shuffles gene segments to create a unique receptor. Your body makes ~1 billion different ones. Cells that match "self" too well are killed during development. The survivors are your repertoire.

Coming up next: Vaccines & Pandemics

Now that you know how immunity works, vaccines are easy. They teach the adaptive system to recognize a threat without ever fighting the real disease.

Module 9 complete
300M
people killed by smallpox in the 20th century — until vaccines eradicated it in 1980.

Vaccines are practice for your immune system.

You already know the adaptive immune system gets faster after the first encounter with an invader. Vaccines exploit that: they give your immune system the "first encounter" without the disease.

By the time the real virus shows up, you already have memory cells ready to respond in hours — fast enough that you barely notice.

Module 10 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

The big idea, in four steps.

Every vaccine — no matter what kind — does the same fundamental thing. Show your immune system something that looks like a real pathogen, without actually being one.

1️⃣
Show a fake invader

The vaccine contains a piece of (or look-alike of) the real pathogen — usually one of its surface proteins.

2️⃣
Immune system reacts

Your body sees something foreign, panics a little (= sore arm, mild fever) and starts building custom antibodies.

3️⃣
Memory cells form

After the practice fight, memory B-cells and T-cells stay behind. They carry the "wanted poster" for years.

4️⃣
Real virus arrives → fast win

When the actual pathogen shows up, memory cells trigger a response in hours. The pathogen is gone before you feel sick.

Scene 3 of 6 · Why we need lots of people vaccinated

Why everyone else getting vaccinated protects you too.

A pathogen needs susceptible hosts to spread. When enough people are immune (either by vaccination or recovery), the pathogen runs out of targets. The chain breaks. This is herd immunity.

📈
R₀ — how contagious?

The basic reproduction number. Average people one sick person infects. Flu: R₀ ≈ 1.3. COVID: ≈ 2–3. Smallpox: ≈ 5–7. Measles: ≈ 15–20 — most contagious of all.

🛡️
Herd threshold

To stop spread, need 1 − 1/R₀ of the population immune. Flu: ~23%. COVID: ~67%. Smallpox: ~83%. Measles: ~95% — that's why outbreaks return when vaccination dips even slightly.

👶 Why herd immunity matters morally.

Some people can't be vaccinated — newborns, the immunocompromised, cancer patients on chemo. They depend on the rest of us being immune so the pathogen can't reach them in the first place. Vaccination is partly a personal choice and partly a debt to your most vulnerable neighbors.

Coming up next: CRISPR & Modern Tools

You've seen how cells, DNA, viruses, and immunity all interconnect. Now meet the technology that lets us read AND rewrite the recipe book directly.

Module 10 complete
2 billion
years ago — when bacteria invented gene editing as anti-virus defense.

CRISPR is bacterial immunity we borrowed.

In 2012, researchers Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier realized something profound: bacteria have a system for cutting viral DNA at exact target sequences. The system worked outside bacteria too — on any DNA, in any cell.

Within 5 years, CRISPR was being used to cure inherited diseases. In 2023, the first CRISPR therapy (for sickle cell) was approved by the FDA.

Module 11 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

The three parts of CRISPR.

CRISPR is a precise genetic scalpel with three components.

🎯
Guide RNA

A short RNA strand designed to match the exact DNA sequence you want to edit. It's the GPS.

✂️
Cas9 protein

A molecular scissors. Cuts both strands of DNA wherever the guide RNA points.

🔧
The cell's repair

Once cut, the cell glues the DNA back together — often imperfectly. That's the edit.

📋
(Optional) repair template

Supply a "use this" template the cell pastes in instead of patching randomly.

Disease-resistant crops, gene drives to suppress malaria-carrying mosquitoes, drought-tolerant varieties.

⚠️ Germline editing is the open question.

Edits to body cells affect only the patient. Edits to germline cells (sperm/egg/embryo) pass to all future descendants. In 2018 a researcher in China edited embryos that grew into babies; the scientific community condemned it. Ethics, safety, and regulation remain unresolved.
