Tiny typos. Long-term consequences.

Every time a cell copies its DNA, it makes a few mistakes. Most of those typos do absolutely nothing — the cell rolls on like it never happened. Sometimes a typo changes one ingredient in one recipe. Once in a while, it stops a recipe in the middle.

Stack those rare changes up across millions of years and billions of organisms, and you get the entire diversity of life on Earth — from algae to elephants to you.

Module 6 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

Three flavors of typo.

When one DNA letter gets swapped for another, it lands in a codon — a 3-letter "word" the ribosome reads. Depending on which word the typo creates, the consequences range from nothing to everything.

🟢
Silent

The codon changed, but the new codon codes for the same amino acid. Protein unchanged. The genetic code's "redundancy" is doing you a favor.

🟡
Missense

The new codon codes for a different amino acid. The protein gets one wrong ingredient. Sometimes harmless, sometimes catastrophic.

🔴
Nonsense

The new codon is a stop signal. The ribosome quits early. The protein is short and almost always broken.

⏳
+ Time + Selection

Tiny advantages from rare beneficial mutations spread. Over millions of years, that's evolution. No designer needed.

Scene 3 of 6 · One famous example

One letter, two stories.

In the gene for hemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in your red blood cells), there's a codon that reads GAG — Glutamic acid. Sometimes a copy error changes that middle letter to a U: GUG — Valine. One letter. One amino acid. Huge consequences.

Two copies of the mutation

Sickle cell disease. Under stress, hemoglobin clumps and bends red blood cells into rigid crescents that get stuck in tiny vessels. Painful, sometimes fatal.

One copy of the mutation

Mostly fine — and partially resistant to malaria. The malaria parasite has a much harder time inside the sickle-shaped cells.

Zero copies

Normal hemoglobin, normal red cells. Fully susceptible to malaria — which is why, in places where malaria is common, carriers actually live longer on average.

🎯 Mutations aren't bad → good. They're traded off.

The sickle-cell mutation looks "bad" in places without malaria, and "good" in places with it. Selection doesn't pick perfect — it picks whatever survives long enough to reproduce. Over millions of generations, that's all evolution is.
