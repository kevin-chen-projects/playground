Viruses are biology's freeloaders.

A virus isn't really alive. It has DNA or RNA wrapped in a protein shell, and that's it — no cells, no metabolism, no way to reproduce on its own.

But give it a living cell to hijack, and it becomes a copy machine. It turns the cell's machinery against itself, makes hundreds of new virus particles, and bursts out to find more cells.

Module 8 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

The basic anatomy of a virus.

Strip a virus down to its essentials. There are only a few parts — and not every virus has all of them.

🧬
Genetic payload

DNA or RNA — usually just a few thousand to a few hundred thousand bases. Tiny.

📦
Capsid (protein shell)

A geometric protein cage that protects the genome until it finds a host.

🛢️
Envelope (sometimes)

A fatty outer layer stolen from a previous host cell. Influenza and HIV have one; many others don't.

🔑
Surface keys

Spike proteins that fit specific lock receptors on host cells — what determines which cells the virus can infect.

Scene 3 of 6 · How viruses vary

Not all viruses are alike.

The category mostly comes down to what's inside the capsid and how that genetic payload gets copied. These three are the main families for human disease.

📘
DNA viruses

Carry DNA. Use the host's normal copy machinery. Slow to mutate (good copying). Examples: herpes, chickenpox, HPV, smallpox.

📕
RNA viruses

Carry RNA. Use a sloppy enzyme (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) that copies fast but makes errors. Fast mutators. Examples: flu, cold, measles, COVID, hepatitis A/C, polio.

📒
Retroviruses

Carry RNA but reverse-transcribe it into DNA, then insert into your genome permanently. Examples: HIV. About 8% of human DNA came from ancient retroviruses long ago.

🌊 Why flu changes every year.

Flu is an RNA virus. Each replication round, the sloppy enzyme makes a few errors. Most are deleterious — but rare ones change the surface keys enough that last year's antibodies don't recognize this year's virus. That's why you need a new flu shot annually.

Viruses in one sentence.
