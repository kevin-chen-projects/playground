CRISPR is bacterial immunity we borrowed.

In 2012, researchers Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier realized something profound: bacteria have a system for cutting viral DNA at exact target sequences. The system worked outside bacteria too — on any DNA, in any cell.

Within 5 years, CRISPR was being used to cure inherited diseases. In 2023, the first CRISPR therapy (for sickle cell) was approved by the FDA.

Module 11 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

The three parts of CRISPR.

CRISPR is a precise genetic scalpel with three components.

🎯
Guide RNA

A short RNA strand designed to match the exact DNA sequence you want to edit. It's the GPS.

✂️
Cas9 protein

A molecular scissors. Cuts both strands of DNA wherever the guide RNA points.

🔧
The cell's repair

Once cut, the cell glues the DNA back together — often imperfectly. That's the edit.

📋
(Optional) repair template

Supply a "use this" template the cell pastes in instead of patching randomly.

Scene 3 of 6 · Real-world impact

What CRISPR is actually doing right now.

From lab curiosity to FDA-approved therapy in 11 years. Here's what it's being used for today.

🩸
Sickle cell (approved 2023)

CRISPR edits the patient's bone marrow stem cells to reactivate fetal hemoglobin. Functional cure in clinical trials.

👁️
Inherited blindness (in trials)

Direct in-vivo editing of retinal cells to fix Leber congenital amaurosis. Some patients have regained sight.

🦠
Cancer immunotherapy

Patient's T-cells extracted, CRISPR-edited to recognize their tumor, reinfused. Already approved for some leukemias.

🌾
Crops & ecology

Disease-resistant crops, gene drives to suppress malaria-carrying mosquitoes, drought-tolerant varieties.

⚠️ Germline editing is the open question.

Edits to body cells affect only the patient. Edits to germline cells (sperm/egg/embryo) pass to all future descendants. In 2018 a researcher in China edited embryos that grew into babies; the scientific community condemned it. Ethics, safety, and regulation remain unresolved.
