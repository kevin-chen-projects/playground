Welcome to the most fun part of Mandarin.

In English, "ma" is just "ma" — your pitch can rise or fall without changing what you mean. In Mandarin, the pitch is the meaning.

Listen — these are 4 real Mandarin words. Same letters. Same consonant. Same vowel. Different pitches → different meanings.

Scene 3 of 6 · Tone pairs are next — that's where the real practice lives

Tone pairs — where Mandarin actually happens.

Most Mandarin words are 2 syllables. So practicing tones in pairs (16 combinations: 1+1, 1+2, 1+3, 1+4, 2+1 …) is more useful than drilling single tones. Click any cell to hear the pair on "ma-ma".

⚡
Tone sandhi: when 3+3 secretly becomes 2+3.

Two 3rd tones in a row are awkward to actually say, so the first one becomes a 2nd tone. 你好 (nǐ + hǎo) is pronounced 你好 (ní + hǎo). Spelling stays; pronunciation shifts. Native speakers do it automatically.

Pinyin: read Mandarin without learning a single character.

Pinyin (拼音, "spell sound") is the romanization system that maps Chinese sounds to letters you already know. Once you can read pinyin, you can pronounce any Chinese word — even ones written in characters you've never seen.

Tones taught you how to say things. Pinyin teaches you what.

📝 Tone mark placement rule.

The tone mark sits on the strongest vowel: a → o → e → i → u → ü, in that order. So "hao" gets it on "a" (hǎo, not haǒ), "hui" gets it on "i" (huì), and "you" gets it on "o" (yòu).

Characters look random. Until you see the pieces.

A Chinese character looks like a maze of strokes — until you realize it's actually a stack of smaller, reusable pieces called radicals. Once you see them, you stop memorizing characters and start reading them.

The big idea: you don't need to memorize 3,000+ characters. You memorize ~50 building blocks and the rules for how they combine.

Module 3 of 11 · Scene 1 of 6

Characters are LEGO.

Most Chinese characters are made of 2 or 3 smaller pieces. Linguists call them radicals or components. Pieces slot together in fixed positions — left/right, top/bottom, or surround.

🧱
~50 building blocks

Most everyday characters are built from a small set of common radicals — about 50 of them cover 80%+ of common characters.

📍
Position is fixed

When 木 (tree) is on the left of a character (e.g. 林, 树), it's always written left. When on bottom (e.g. 杏), always bottom. Recognizing position helps you find parts.

🖼️
Pictographs (oldest)

Some radicals look like the thing they mean: 山 mountain, 月 moon, 木 tree, 火 fire. The simplest characters started as drawings.

🔊
Phonetic + semantic

Most modern characters combine a meaning radical + a sound radical. 妈 (mā mom) = 女 (woman, meaning) + 马 (mǎ horse, sound).

Scene 3 of 6 · How they combine

3 ways characters are built.

Once you know the building blocks, the next thing is how they combine. Three patterns dominate. Tap any example to hear it spoken.

1
Pictographs (~10% of common chars)

The character looks like the thing. Often the oldest characters in the language.

2
Ideographs (~5%)

Two simple meanings combined into a new meaning. Like rebus puzzles.

3
Phonetic-semantic compounds (~80% — most common)

One radical hints at meaning; another hints at sound. The vast majority of modern characters work this way.
